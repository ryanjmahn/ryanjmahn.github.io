Two projects, one question: when a computational result has no outside referee, what makes it trustworthy?

  • paper

    Auditing the evaluation, not just the result.

    Accepted for presentation at NeurIPS 2026 — TAE workshop and AI4Science workshop · co-authored

    We built a method for catching a silent failure in ancestral protein reconstruction, then turned the scrutiny on ourselves and audited the evaluation protocol we had used to test it. The audit surfaced several ways a protocol can be locally correct and still globally misleading: weak baselines, too-thin controls, unchecked conventions and silent hardware errors.

    In scientific machine learning there is often one dataset, one lab and no outside referee, so failures like these can make it into print unnoticed. The paper closes with a practical checklist any lab can run against its own protocol, with no domain expertise required.

  • benchmark

    Independent Research — Hanyang University, mentored by Professor Se-Jin Hwang (2026–present)

    The specialist tool lost on its own home turf.

    Manuscript under review, Columbia Junior Science Journal · sole author

    Benchmarking three MD-free pocket predictors on the KRAS G12D Switch-II pocket · code and data public

    What I did. KRAS was called undruggable for thirty years. What changed was a cryptic pocket — a binding site absent from the protein's resting shape that forms only as it flexes. Because such pockets are invisible in the structure a lab actually has, researchers rely on software to find them, and most of that software was built on pockets you can already see.

    I benchmarked three tools on the Switch-II pocket of KRAS G12D, the mutation behind most pancreatic cancers: fpocket (pure geometry, no machine learning), P2Rank (general-purpose machine learning), and AE-PocketMiner (a graph neural network built specifically for cryptic pockets). Each ran on two crystal structures bracketing the pocket's opening and on a 24-step ladder of intermediate structures generated from the closed one alone, with no molecular dynamics.

    What I found.

    • The specialist came last. On the real open structure, pure geometry localized the pocket best (0.92 recall, 0.95 MCC) and the general-purpose model second (0.72, 0.83), both at perfect precision. The cryptic-pocket specialist flagged 45 residues for 0.40 recall at 0.12 MCC — it found the pocket without localizing it.
    • The closed structure reversed the ranking. Where the pocket doesn't exist, only the general-purpose model correctly returned nothing; the geometric tool reported one anyway.
    • Volume isn't enough. Four conformers at open-state volume returned no pocket at the site while neighbours of the same volume were found.
    • Detection rate alone would have hidden all of it. By that metric the specialist "succeeded" and the general-purpose model "failed" on the closed structure. Both readings are backwards.

    One target and one pocket, so this shows the expected ranking can fail on a clinically important case, not that it fails as a rule. Conformers come from normal mode analysis alone. The openness axis is produced by one of the tools being tested, which doesn't affect scoring but isn't independent.

    • 0.95 / 0.12MCC for the geometric tool and the cryptic-pocket specialist on the real open structure
    • 245 → 822 Å³pocket volume, closed crystal structure to open
    • 0.96 Åredocking accuracy of the known drug in the open crystal structure, verified before any benchmarking

    Methods integrity. The open structure is the answer key, so it never touched the pipeline that generates the test data — every conformer comes from the closed structure alone. Before any comparison, I re-docked the known drug into the open structure and confirmed it landed within 0.96 Å of its crystal pose: a pipeline that can't reproduce a known answer can't be trusted on an unknown one. All three tools are scored by one function over the same residue set, and every value regenerates from committed data.
