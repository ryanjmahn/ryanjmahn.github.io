Two projects, one question: when a computational result has no outside referee, what makes it trustworthy?

  • paper

    Contaminated Ancestors: Seven Ways Our Own Evaluation Protocol Nearly Told Us the Wrong Thing

    NeurIPS 2026 · TAE workshop · accepted

    What we were doing. Ancestral reconstruction infers what a protein looked like millions of years ago, assuming every lineage has one parent. When two histories have merged, the method doesn't fail loudly — it returns a confident chimera. We built a detector for that, then wrote the paper about our evaluation instead of our result.

    What we found. Our detector flagged 2 of 15 contaminated cases — and 2 of 15 in a control with nothing to find, because a calibrated test is supposed to fire at its threshold on empty data. What really separated signal from noise was where the flag landed: 81% overlap with the true block, versus 8% for the control. Six more failures followed, from an unchecked sign convention that inverted a score (0.048 where the truth was 0.952) to seed noise larger than every effect we studied. Plain string comparison even beat the neural network (36/40 vs 11/40), and a laptop GPU silently turned p = 0.030 into p = 1.000.

    Why it matters. Scientific ML often has one dataset, one lab, a few seeds, and nobody outside positioned to check. A protocol can be locally correct and globally misleading, and still get published. The paper closes with an 11-question checklist any lab can run on its own protocol — none of it requires the biology.

    • 2/15 vs 2/15detection rate on real contamination versus on nothing at all
    • 0.048 → 0.952a score inverted by an unchecked sign convention
    • 11questions in the resulting protocol checklist

    Independent Research — Hanyang University, mentored by Professor Se-Jin Hwang (2026–present)

    The specialist tool lost on its own home turf.

    Benchmarking three MD-free pocket predictors on the KRAS G12D Switch-II cryptic pocket · code and data public

    What I did. KRAS was called undruggable for thirty years. What changed was the discovery of a cryptic pocket — a binding site absent from the protein's resting shape that only forms as the protein flexes. The tools researchers use to hunt such sites were mostly built on pockets you can already see, so I tested how three of them behave on one that isn't there yet.

    I built a 24-step ladder of structures running from closed to open, generated from the closed structure alone by normal mode analysis, with no molecular dynamics anywhere. That turns "how open is the pocket" into a measurable axis rather than a label. I ran three tools across every rung and on both real experimental structures: fpocket (pure geometry, the floor), P2Rank (general-purpose machine learning, what most labs already use), and PocketMiner (machine learning built specifically for cryptic pockets, the specialist).

    What I found.

    • P2Rank localized it best. On the real open structure it recovered the pocket at 0.80 recall and 0.61 MCC, and treated it as one unified site rather than pieces.
    • fpocket fragmented it. The geometric baseline found the region but split the extended pocket into disconnected sub-pockets, averaging about 0.25 recall across the ladder, and its predicted center sat offset from the true site.
    • PocketMiner over-predicted. The specialist flagged 40+ residues at its 0.7 threshold — a large share of the whole protein — so it swept up true residues without localizing them, scoring 0.12 MCC. Its confidence on the true pocket residues barely exceeded its protein-wide average, and the same behavior held on the real open structure, so it isn't an artifact of my generated conformers.
    • Detection tracked openness. Both pocket-based tools improved as the pocket opened, confirming the ladder captured real pocket formation; PocketMiner stayed flat, consistent with flagging broadly regardless of shape.

    The counterintuitive result is the one worth keeping: the tool built for cryptic pockets localized this cryptic pocket worse than the general-purpose one. Cryptic detection is the capability that made KRAS druggable, and KRAS G12D drives treatment-resistant pancreatic cancer — so which tool a lab reaches for on a hard target is a decision with consequences, and specialization turned out to be assumed rather than verified.

    One target and one pocket, so this is not a general claim about any tool. Conformers come from normal mode analysis alone, without further refinement. Comparing a tool that returns per-residue probabilities against tools that return pockets requires a threshold, which is a choice I made — though the result holds without it, since the specialist's confidence on the true pocket residues barely exceeds its average across the whole protein. And KRAS is genuinely flexible in more than one region, which may account for part of the broad flagging.

    • 0.80 / 0.61recall and MCC for the general-purpose tool on the real open structure
    • 0.12MCC for the tool built specifically for cryptic pockets
    • 245 → 822 Å³pocket volume, closed crystal structure to open

    Methods integrity. The open structure is the answer key, so it never touched the pipeline that produces the test data: every conformer is built from the closed structure only, and the open one sits in a folder named so it can't be used by accident. Before any tool comparison, I re-docked the known drug into the real open structure and confirmed it landed within 0.96 Å of its crystal pose. If the pipeline can't reproduce a known answer, nothing downstream of it means anything.
